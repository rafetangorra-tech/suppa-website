about

Stop asking
for permission

Long Island produces a specific kind of ambition. It's the kind that comes from growing up an hour from Manhattan and knowing exactly how close the whole thing is. Suppa has had it since she was a kid singing for a living room full of relatives — and she's never once talked about it like a maybe.

She's a professional makeup artist by trade, a detail that tracks the second you hear her music: everything is deliberate, nothing is accidental, and the finish matters as much as the bones underneath.

The songs live in the space between a dance floor and a car parked outside someone's house at 2 a.m. Big, sticky pop hooks built on top of writing that actually says something — the kind that has you screaming a chorus and then quietly realizing it's about you. She has the emotional range of someone who's lived a few more decades than her ID says, and she's not interested in sanding that down to fit a lane. Vulnerable, glamorous, a little unhinged, fully in control — she'll be all of it, sometimes in the same three minutes.

Her tight-knit Italian family and her Long Island roots are the anchor. And the reason she's doing this is simple: the girl in her bedroom who's been told her plan is unrealistic. Suppa wants that girl to hear one of her songs and stop asking for permission.
